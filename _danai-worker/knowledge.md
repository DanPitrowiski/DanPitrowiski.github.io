Q: Can you walk me through your design process?
A: Sure thing! This is my typical process:
1. Know the Problem: The first step is always understanding the full problem, know the business goals, technical constraints, and user research.
2. Ideas: With the team, developers, and/or stakeholders talk about potential solutions and lay them out.
3. Rapid Prototyping: Using tools like Cursor, Claude, Figma Make we can generate our ideas quickly.
4. Testing: Get the prototypes in front of people and start hearing feedback. Iterate fast.
5. Internal Feedback: Show the prototypes to the team and see if there are any last changes or concerns. It’s easier for people to give feedback the more concrete ideas are.
6. Hand-off: Get the design ready for hand-off to the developers.
This type of flow I find best to get an idea, to design, and to production with the best results.

Q: How do you design AI people can trust?
A: There are (3) keys I learned from building an AI product for Wootric CXI.
1. Transparent Results - show users exactly how the AI did the work and what it changed or added.
2. User Controls - Give the user as many guardrails and controls as they need to make sure the AI is only working the way they want it to.
3. Case Study - Build a real-life use case with one customer at least, that can be an example for others. It's easier to trust you if one customer already vouches for you.

Overview

## What is Wootric?

Wootric started as a survey company. Any business could drop a snippet of code into its website, mobile app, text or email and start collecting NPS, CSAT, or CES responses in minutes.

CXInsights (CXI) is the enterprise layer built on top of it. It pulls feedback from every survey source into one place, lets teams build their own dashboards, and uses AI to sort open-ended feedback into themes with sentiment, so a CX team can see what customers are saying at a scale no person could read.

The problem

## Problems

01

Enterprise customers sometimes received millions of responses a month. DocuSign, Zoom and Glassdoor had teams exporting spreadsheets and building dashboards by hand.

Data sent with surveys was disorganized. One source passed an empty company_name, another passed organization or account_name. People filtered on these and saw a blank page.

Customers see value as soon as feedback is loaded.

### Source of truth

Custom dashboards without a BI tool or spreadsheet.

Principles

## Design thinking

- Simple. Customers have plenty of places to analyze data. CXI had to give them value from day one.

- Actionable. Don’t just show the data. Tell the customer what they can act on.

- Transparent AI. Every machine-applied tag says how and why it was applied, right where it appears.

- Meet people where they report. CX managers live in executive decks, so dashboards had to be clean enough to screenshot.

- Big, dirty data. Inconsistent schemas, missing metadata, mislabeled fields. The experience had to work around all of it.

Landscape

## Competitive analysis

### Tableau

Powerful and technical. Customers needed an in-house analyst to use it.

### Spreadsheets

Manual and slow, but the most common way customers handled feedback.

### Small AI shops

Custom models per customer. Expensive, with little industry knowledge.

Exploration

## Experiments

### Categorizing feedback with tags

ShippedCut or postponed

- Shipped: Smart Tags. Matched automatically by machine learning, with a tag set for each industry built with customers.

- Cut: Deleting Smart Tags to train the model. Retraining needed 1,000 to 5,000 clean responses and a rebuild, so corrections stayed on the response.

- Shipped: Text Match Tags. Users add specific phrases, like “credit card” for a BILLING tag.

- Cut: Text Match Tags with filters. Only tag feedback matching a custom filter. After scoping with engineering, it moved to a later release.

- Shipped: Manual Tags. Applied by hand, one response at a time.

- Cut: Sentiment-only tags. I argued sentiment should live outside of tagging.

[Image: Hover cards for Smart, Text Match and Manual tags, each showing tag sentiment and how the tag was applied]

Hover cards explain how each tag was applied and what sentiment it carries.

### Survey feedback cards

Feedback cards went through the most iterations. They had to be easy to scan, look different where it mattered (score type, sentiment), and make tags quick to add, delete, and understand.

[Image: Feedback card exploration: a feedback breakdown chart beside NPS, CSAT and CES feedback cards with tags]

Feedback card explorations, done quickly while sitting with the team.

### Rapid prototyping

- Cut: AI controls. Letting users tune the sentiment threshold was too confusing. Two wrong responses in a hundred made people want to change the whole model, so we let them fix one response at a time instead.

- Shipped: Custom reports. We built the first reports with customers, reproducing the reports they most often made for themselves.

- Cut: Onboarding and empty states. My design intern built a great onboarding flow. We pivoted when account managers said they would preload data and onboard enterprise customers personally.

Context

## What is CXI?

CXInsights (CXI) was Wootric’s app for analyzing open-ended survey feedback at scale.

Each comment was tagged by topic, like UX/UI or ONBOARDING. Every tag got its own sentiment score, and so did the comment as a whole. We grouped sentiment into Negative, Neutral, and Positive.

The problem

## Customers didn’t trust the AI

“I think the AI could be better.”

Anonymous G2 customer review

Customers didn’t have confidence in AI to do auto-tagging and apply sentiment correctly.

For our Enterprise customers who had millions of responses, they wanted all their responses in one place with the AI run on top of it. So we needed a solution that scales.

[Image: A feedback card with a Smart Tag and its positive and negative tag sentiments]

Automatically tag feedback and apply sentiment scores in a way that gives customers confidence it’s been done correctly.

### Why we said no

- Retraining was expensive.

### Why we said no

- Sentiment was Negative, Neutral, or Positive, but we set the score ranges that decided each one.

- In testing, exposing these settings added more complication and questions than it helped.

## Giving customers control without retraining

### More auto-tagging control

- Scoped tags. Tags can apply only to feedback that matches specific filters.

- Text Match tags. Cover what Smart Tags missed, using phrases the customer chooses.

- Tag descriptions. Shown on hover, so customers define their own tagging system and see it where they need it.

Managing tags: scope, type, and description.

- Translation toggle. Turn translations on and off.

- Inline translations. Added directly onto the feedback, so it’s easy to read in context.

Proof

## AI vs. humans

Trust needed evidence, so we ran a test with a CXI customer.

- The test
Five people on the customer’s team each tagged the same 500 new pieces of feedback. We compared their tags to our model’s on those 500.

- Results
(1) The team was inconsistent, catching some tags and missing others. (2) The model tagged everything the team caught, plus a couple they missed. (3) All five team members combined were only as accurate as the model.

- Conclusion
The model’s error rate was so small it was statistically insignificant for customers with millions of responses, and it saved them a huge amount of time. Wootric used this case study often.

## Overview

Project 1 of 4

## Using AI to close the loop

### Problem

Enterprise customers collect millions of survey responses, reviews, and support conversations. Teams couldn't tell which ones were worth following up on. When they did follow up, it happened in other tools, so nobody in the CX platform could see whether a customer's feedback had actually been addressed.

### Key decision

I redesigned how AI insights were surfaced and acted on, around two goals:

- Close the loop faster: Get customers doing their job faster.

- Track It: We wanted to track the follow-up and push that data to integrated apps for others to see.

### What shipped

AI insights pick out the highest-impact responses from a pool of millions, so teams know where reaching out will matter most. The same analysis works on conversations, tagging what the customer said and how the agent responded.

[Image: A contact-center conversation with AI-detected insights on the left, such as Complaint, Quality, and Sadness, and agent attributes on the right, such as Attitude Apologetic]

AI analysis. Customer insights on the left, agent behavior on the right, each linked to the exact words that triggered it.

For public reviews, I designed AI-drafted replies in the brand's voice: the team edits the draft, then copies it to the review site. When Qualtrics acquired Press Ganey, Wootric's parent company, new features like this one were put on hold. The designs were approved, but the full implementation was never completed.

[Image: A five-star Google review with keyword analysis and an AI-generated draft response]

The approved design for an AI-drafted response to a Google review, with editable AI rules and a reminder to proofread before posting.

Every response gets a workflow for following up: tag it, add a note, reply, forward, and mark it complete.

[Image: A survey response with tags, an internal note being edited, and reply, forward, and mark complete actions]

Closing the loop on a single response: tags, an internal note, and the actions that sync back to the platform.

### Result

Customers are building these steps into their everyday workflow. The biggest change is a sharp drop in the manual work that used to come with finding insights and closing the loop, and customers now want to go further, refining the process and measuring the results.

Project 2 of 4

## Unified customer data across platforms

### Problem

Customer data was split across Salesforce, Intercom, our own database, Recurly, JIRA, and scattered spreadsheets. Most of it was only visible to the individual account manager who owned it, so nobody had a company-wide picture of who our customers were or how they were doing.

### Key decision

I designed a unified customer database around the people who would use it: Sales looking for upsell targets, Product needing a platform-wide view, and Customer Support needing full account context. The hardest design choice was linking individual accounts to their parent organizations, which had never been done before. We used Holistics, a self-service BI platform, so teams could query and build dashboards without waiting on an analyst.

### What shipped

Six disconnected systems consolidated into one shared customer database, queryable with SQL and visible company-wide.

### Result

For the first time, Press Ganey had a company-wide view of the Wootric platform, and the database became a foundation for cross-functional teams. The new visibility led directly to upsell opportunities with customers likely to pay more, and recovered sales from demo accounts that had fallen through the cracks.

Project 3 of 4

## Custom survey designs across web, email, iOS, and Android

### Problem

Customers wanted surveys that matched their brand everywhere they appeared. Making one design look and behave the same in every email client and in native iOS and Android apps is much harder than it sounds, and every combination a customer might choose still had to meet accessibility standards.

[Image: A branded mobile banking survey with follow-up questions shown across several screens]

One branded survey flow on mobile, from rating to follow-up questions to an app store prompt.

### Key decision

Rather than asking customers to trust that a design would carry over, the admin previews the same survey on each channel, and on desktop and mobile, and all email clients, before it goes live.

The biggest constraint on consistency was email platforms and older mobile devices. Microsoft Outlook gave our rounded buttons enough trouble that we had to settle on square. For mobile devices, we had to set a cut-off on iOS and Androids with less than 0.1% of users.

### What shipped

[Image: The survey design admin switching between in-app web, email, iOS, and Android previews]

The design admin. Switch channels and screen sizes to see exactly how a survey will look.

### Result

Several customers were extremely excited about the update. They had been considering other products for their customizations, and we now gave them enough options to keep them from churning.

## Web survey accessibility

### Problem

Customers choose their own brand colors, and many of those choices make survey text hard to read. With surveys reaching people in more than 80 countries, unreadable text means lost responses and excluded customers.

### Key decision

We set a goal to meet WCAG AAA across the platform, from surveys to the admin to dashboards, and made contrast automatic so customers couldn't accidentally break it.

### What shipped

The survey picks black or white text automatically based on the background color the customer sets. In the rare cases where contrast still falls short, the admin shows a warning saying whether the design meets AA or AAA.

Together, these projects moved the platform from collecting feedback to acting on it. ## Lessons from the concourse

### The moment

During a University of Miami vs. Florida State baseball weekend in April 2013, our team and student volunteers worked the stadium to get fans to download the app, then wrote up what worked.

### What we learned

- Kids open the door. Free mini sport balls with a U on them had children begging parents to download the app, and the parents usually liked it once they heard more.

- Be a fan, not a marketer. One volunteer joined the milkshake line and got four people around him to download the app. Fans relaxed when the pitch came from another ballpark-goer.

- Place collateral where people pause. Fliers handed out at the main entrance worked because fans finished reading just as they reached the table.

- Avoid where people rush. Seating-area entrances failed. Everyone was hurrying to their seats, the food line, or the bathroom between innings.

- Luck helps. A Friday rain delay kept everyone on the concourse, and nearly all of that night's downloads happened during the delay.

### What we found, and what I'd do today

- Finding
The app asked for location and push notification permission back to back, before people had even used it.

Today
Ask in context. Request location when someone taps "Find teams near me," and notifications after they've followed a team.

- Finding
Alerts and full-screen modals interrupted people constantly, even for good news like earning points.

Today
Save alerts for real errors. Use a light, dismissible toast for points, like Facebook's "New stories" pill.

- Finding
People struggled to find Redeem, and after submitting a code the page stayed open as if something else needed doing.

Today
Make Redeem a first-class destination, and confirm with a clear message like "15% off redeemed. Your certificate is on its way."

- Finding
The profile screen was pushed onto the Feed tab's navigation stack, so a tester who tapped Feed later landed back on Profile and got lost.

Today
Give Profile its own tab or open it as a modal, and never mix tab and stack navigation in ways people can't predict.

- Finding
The event feed showed hundreds of identical "Person X will be watching" updates.

Today
Group activity and show friends first, with everyone else collapsed into a single line.

Most of these are now standard mobile best practice. In 2013 we learned them the hard way, by watching people get stuck.

## Moderating fan photos

### The moment

Photo challenges were one of the most popular features, and some of the most abused. Some fans uploaded random or inappropriate photos just to collect points. We had no tools to review photos or deal with repeat offenders, so the team was sending warning emails by hand.

[Image: An internal page showing recent fan photo uploads in a Pinterest-style grid, with faces blurred and three flagged photos hidden]

The first internal prototype: every recent upload in one scrolling wall. Faces blurred; flagged examples obscured for this case study.

### The debate

An engineer prototyped a Pinterest-style infinite scroll of every upload, which made bad photos obvious at a glance. Our lead engineer pushed back. For an admin tool, a uniform grid is faster and more precise: with variable-height columns you can't predict where the controls will land. He also warned that reviewing every photo before it posted would be a huge headache.

My view was that we were solving two different problems. The real damage came from people stealing points, and only secondarily from inappropriate images. So I proposed rewarding only challenges completed correctly, and holding questionable photos until they were cleared. I also pointed out that the same tool could do something positive: flag great photos to feature in marketing, ads, and the app itself.

### What shipped

We agreed on a short, prioritized list: flag a photo for deletion, link to the user to see or delete all of their photos or their account, and flag a photo to feature. The first piece was deploying within two days.

Featuring great photos was already working for us. Weekly photo contests were some of our best marketing.

## Advice for Other Designers

- Lead with the result. If you have a number, put it in the title or the first line.

- Make your hero line count. Add a result, who you helped, or your specialty. If the hero just mentions you being a product designer, it won't stand out.

- Tell me what's behind the click. Your case study cards should say what the product is, what you worked on and how it turned out. Don't make someone click just to find out.

- Aim for a five-minute case study. The average case study is about 1,100 words. If yours is pushing 2,000, it's probably time to edit. (This is a problem I have.)

- Name the hard decision. Over half the case studies describe a trade-off. The strongest put it right in the title, like Alex Chiu's “Challenging the Brief.”

- Show the work. Sketches, whiteboards, rejected designs. Seeing the process made for the strongest case studies. If you took a photo of your sketches, use it.

- Link your resume. Fewer than half the homepages do. Why not?

- It's never finished. I don't think any portfolio ever is. Treat it like a product: research it, ship it, and keep improving it.

## The goal

From the feedback I gathered, the feature people wanted most was cool skills to use. adjHero: function () {
hero.accuracy += keeneye.statAdj;
},
negHero: function () {
hero.accuracy -= keeneye.statAdj;
},
};

The hardest part was tracking active skills and resetting the character when a skill ran out. That's what the adjHero and negHero functions are for: one applies the bonus, the other removes it.
